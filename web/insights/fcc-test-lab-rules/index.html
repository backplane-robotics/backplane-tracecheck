New FCC rules on where radio devices may be tested: what robotics makers need to know

From December 1, 2028, the FCC will no longer accept testing for radio-equipment certification from test labs in countries that do not have a reciprocal agreement with the United States. Robots that use Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, cellular or other radios need FCC equipment authorization, so manufacturers who test in those countries have about two years to move future testing. Products already certified are not affected.

Plain-language summary of FCC Order 26-73. Not legal advice. Numbers in [brackets] are paragraph numbers in the order.

What the FCC decided

  • Test labs. The FCC will only recognize test labs located in the United States or in a “Reciprocal Economy”: an economy that has a mutual recognition agreement (MRA) or a comparable reciprocal trade agreement with the U.S. covering equipment authorization [3, 39; Appendix A, § 2.902].
  • Certification bodies (TCBs) and laboratory accreditation bodies get the same location rule. The FCC notes that none of them is currently located outside these economies, so for them this mostly writes existing practice into the rules [37, 110, 113].
  • Where the work happens matters, not just the address. An organization cannot keep a recognized site in a Reciprocal Economy while doing the FCC testing, certification or accreditation work elsewhere [115]. The rules call this work “covered activity” [116; Appendix A]:
    • Test lab: setting up and running the tests, the required measurements, analysing the results, and preparing or approving the test report used for certification.
    • TCB: evaluating the application, technical review, the certification decision, the grant or dismissal, and FCC-required post-market surveillance.
    • Accreditation body: assessing, evaluating and deciding to accredit a lab for FCC purposes.
  • What is not covered [117; Appendix A]: pre-compliance or R&D testing that is not presented as FCC testing and is not used in a certification application; making, maintaining or calibrating test equipment; administration such as sales, accounting or IT; and parts of a global company that are kept completely separate from its FCC work. A lab doing FCC testing in a Reciprocal Economy is not disqualified just because it also tests for other countries’ approvals elsewhere [117].
  • No general waiver process for labs outside Reciprocal Economies. The FCC keeps its normal authority to grant waivers for good cause, but says it does not expect to need it here [125].

Why: The FCC says that about five of every six radio devices are now tested in economies without such an agreement [1], and that the share tested in mainland China rose from about 13% in 2007 to more than 82% by 2025 [fn. 326]. It cites oversight, enforcement, falsified test results and over-concentration of testing as its concerns [1, 38; Appendix B ¶ 3].

What does not change

  • Equipment certified before December 1, 2028 stays certified. It may continue to be imported, marketed, sold, operated and modified under the existing rules, without retesting just because its test lab lost recognition [124]. (Retesting can still be required for other reasons, such as inaccurate test information [fn. 310].)
  • Applications received before December 1, 2028 are not disturbed [39].

Which economies count?

The order lists about 60 Reciprocal Economies in Appendix D, including Canada, Mexico, the United Kingdom, the EU member states, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore, Vietnam and Australia. Mainland China is not on the list. The list is not fixed: OET will publish it on its website, review it regularly, and can add or remove economies [39]. Check OET’s current list rather than relying on this summary.

This is not the Covered List

These rules are separate from the FCC Covered List restrictions that we track on our Covered List robot tracker.

A robot can be affected by both, by one, or by neither. For example, an approved robot that is exempt from the Covered List still needs future certification testing done by a lab in the U.S. or a Reciprocal Economy.

Still only proposed

The order also asks for public comment on further measures. These are not rules yet [4, 126–128]:

  • Banning labs, TCBs and accreditation bodies that are owned or controlled by entities in economies without a reciprocal agreement. The FCC considered this and did not adopt it, but kept the question open.
  • Requiring a Legal Entity Identifier (LEI) for labs, TCBs and accreditation bodies.
  • Whether equipment authorized through the Supplier’s Declaration of Conformity (SDoC) process should also be tested in U.S. or Reciprocal Economy labs.
  • Stronger post-market surveillance, protection of manufacturers’ intellectual property during testing, and modernizing the FCC’s Equipment Authorization System.

Comments are due 30 days after Federal Register publication, and replies 60 days after.

A practical checklist for robot makers

  1. List your radios. For each product, note which radios it contains and whether it has its own FCC certification or relies on certified modules.
  2. Know where your testing happens. For each certification you plan to file on or after December 1, 2028, confirm that the test lab and the TCB are located in the U.S. or a Reciprocal Economy, and that the FCC test work itself is done there.
  3. Plan early. The FCC expects labs in the U.S. and Reciprocal Economies to add capacity during the transition, and says it will monitor whether manufacturers can get timely access [fn. 295]. Booking new labs and qualifying them as vendors takes time.
  4. Keep your records. Keep the grants and test reports for products certified before the cut-off. They remain valid.
  5. Watch for updates: OET’s list of Reciprocal Economies, OET’s implementation guidance, and the outcome of the open proposals above.
  6. Check the Covered List separately. Covered List status is a different question; see our tracker.
